Sicily is the largest island in the Mediterranean and one of the most extraordinary destinations in Europe — a place where Greek temples stand above the sea, an active volcano dominates the eastern skyline, Arab-Norman cathedrals rise from Baroque city centres, and the street food is among the most complex and flavourful in Italy. It is also, for now, significantly less visited than the mainland — which means lower prices, fewer crowds, and the sense of discovering something that feels genuinely yours.

Why Sicily Deserves More Than a Week

Sicily has been invaded, occupied, and transformed by Greeks, Carthaginians, Romans, Arabs, Normans, Spanish, and Bourbons — and each culture left something behind. The result is an island of staggering cultural layering: Greek temples older than the Parthenon, Arab-Norman churches with Byzantine mosaics and Islamic geometric patterns, Baroque city centres rebuilt after the 1693 earthquake, and a cuisine that absorbed spices, techniques, and ingredients from every passing civilisation.

It is also large — larger than most visitors expect. Driving from Palermo to Catania takes 2.5 hours; from Palermo to Agrigento, 2 hours. A week is the minimum to do justice to the west and east. Ten days allows you to breathe.

Top Attractions in Sicily

1

Valley of the Temples

Nine Greek temples from the 5th century BC, standing in a UNESCO-listed archaeological park above the southern coast. The Temple of Concordia is the best-preserved Greek temple outside Greece itself. Visit at sunset when the honey-coloured stone glows — and visit early morning to avoid tour groups.

2

Mount Etna

Europe's largest active volcano — and the most accessible. Cable car from Rifugio Sapienza reaches 2,500m; guided hikes go to the crater rim at 3,300m. The landscape of black lava fields, craters, and pine forests is unlike anything else in Italy. Check activity reports before hiking near the summit craters.

3

Taormina & the Greek Theatre

Sicily's most spectacular setting — a hilltop town above the Ionian Sea with a 3rd-century BC Greek theatre that frames Mount Etna behind the stage. One of the most photogenic views in Italy. Taormina itself is tourist-heavy but beautiful; the beach below (Isola Bella) is one of Sicily's finest.

4

Syracuse & Ortigia

Once the largest city in the ancient world, Syracuse is now a manageable and beautiful Baroque city. The island of Ortigia — connected to the mainland by two bridges — contains a Greek temple embedded in the walls of the cathedral, a Caravaggio painting, and some of Sicily's best seafood restaurants.

The Val di Noto — Baroque Perfection

After the 1693 earthquake destroyed southeastern Sicily, eight towns were rebuilt entirely in the exuberant Sicilian Baroque style — Noto, Ragusa, Modica, Scicli, and four others, all now UNESCO-listed. Noto in particular, with its honey-coloured limestone facades and symmetrical Corso Vittorio Emanuele, is one of the most beautiful streets in Italy.

Palermo — The Chaotic, Magnificent Capital

Palermo is unlike any other Italian city — loud, layered, slightly dilapidated, and completely captivating. The Arab-Norman architecture (a UNESCO World Heritage fusion of Islamic, Byzantine, and Norman styles) is extraordinary. The street food markets are among the most intense sensory experiences in Italy. And the city's energy — frantic, warm, and entirely its own — is impossible to replicate.

Palermo's Street Food Markets

The Ballarò and Vucciria markets are as close as Italy gets to a North African souk — dense, noisy, and overwhelming in the best possible way. Street food vendors sell arancini (fried rice balls), sfincione (thick Sicilian pizza), pani ca' meusa (spleen sandwiches — Palermo's most challenging delicacy), and fresh seafood grilled on the spot.

Go to Ballarò for breakfast — the market starts at dawn and the stigghiola (grilled intestines) vendors set up early. Not for the faint-hearted, but completely authentic.

Arab-Norman Palermo — What Not to Miss

The Palatine Chapel inside the Norman Palace has the finest Arab-Norman mosaics in the world — gold Byzantine mosaics covering every surface of a chapel built by Norman kings and decorated by Arab craftsmen in the 12th century. The Martorana church and the Zisa palace complete the picture. All three are covered by one combined ticket.

A 7-Day Sicily Itinerary

Days 1–2

Palermo — Markets, Mosaics & Street Food

Arrive in Palermo. Day 1: Palatine Chapel and the Norman Palace, then the Ballarò market for lunch and street food. Afternoon: the Martorana church and Piazza Pretoria. Day 2: the Cathedral, the Zisa palace, and the Capuchin Catacombs (8,000 mummified bodies displayed in the vaults beneath the city — extraordinary and unmissable). Evening: dinner in the Vucciria district.

Days 3–4

Agrigento & the Val di Noto

Drive south to Agrigento (2 hours). Visit the Valley of the Temples in the late afternoon for the best light. Day 4: continue east through the Val di Noto Baroque towns — stop in Noto (the most beautiful), Modica (famous for its chocolate made to an Aztec recipe brought by the Spanish), and Ragusa Ibla (a perfectly preserved Baroque hill town). Stay overnight in Ragusa or Syracuse.

Days 5–6

Syracuse & Taormina

Morning in Syracuse — the Neapolis Archaeological Park (Greek theatre, Roman amphitheatre) and the island of Ortigia for lunch and the cathedral. Afternoon: drive north to Taormina (1.5 hours). Day 6: the Greek Theatre in Taormina at opening time, then the beach at Isola Bella. Evening: the best views of Etna from Taormina's terraces at sunset.

Day 7

Mount Etna

Full day on Europe's most active volcano. Cable car from Rifugio Sapienza to 2,500m, then either a guided crater hike (3,300m, 3–4 hours) or a walk through the lava fields and minor craters accessible without a guide. The landscape shifts completely with altitude — from orange groves and vineyards at the base to stark black lava fields at the summit. Book a guide for the upper craters through Etna Experience or Guide Alpine Etna.

Sicilian Food — A Cuisine Unlike Any Other

Arancini

Fried rice balls — ragù, mozzarella, or pistachio. Sicily's greatest street food.

Cannoli

Crispy pastry shells filled with ricotta and pistachios. Never pre-filled — order fresh.

Modica Chocolate

Cold-processed chocolate from an ancient Aztec recipe — grainy, intense, extraordinary.

Fresh Seafood

Swordfish from the Strait of Messina, red prawns from Mazara, sea urchin pasta.

  • Pasta alla Norma: Catania's contribution to Italian cuisine — rigatoni with aubergine, tomato, ricotta salata, and basil. Named after Bellini's opera.
  • Granita: Sicily's answer to gelato — a semi-frozen drink of almonds, coffee, lemon, or pistachio, traditionally eaten for breakfast with a brioche. The almond granita from Caffè Sicilia in Noto is legendary.
  • Nero d'Avola: Sicily's great red wine grape — full-bodied, rich, and excellent value. Order it anywhere in the south.
  • Street food rule: Follow the locals. If a vendor has a queue of Sicilians and no English menu on display, you're in the right place.

Getting Around Sicily

A rental car is essential for seeing Sicily properly. The island is large, the train network is slow and limited to coastal routes, and the best archaeological sites, Baroque towns, and inland landscapes are only accessible by road. Fly into Palermo and out of Catania (or vice versa) to avoid backtracking, and allow at least 7 days.

Practical Tips

  • Sicily is significantly cheaper than northern Italy — accommodation, food, and activities all cost less. A mid-range dinner with wine rarely exceeds €30 per person outside tourist areas.
  • The best time to visit Etna's summit craters is July to October when snow has cleared. The cable car runs year-round but upper hiking trails may be closed in winter.
  • Noto's famous Baroque facades are best photographed in the golden hour before sunset, when the honey limestone glows orange. Plan your visit accordingly.
  • Sicilian summers (July–August) are extremely hot — 35–40°C is common inland. June and September offer the same sunshine with far more comfortable temperatures.
  • The Aeolian Islands (Lipari, Stromboli, Vulcano) are reachable by hydrofoil from Milazzo — an outstanding extension to a Sicily trip if time allows.
