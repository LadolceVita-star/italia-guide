Sardinia is unlike any other part of Italy. It is older than Rome, stranger than Sicily, wilder than anywhere on the mainland — a Mediterranean island with a distinct culture, language, and cuisine that developed largely in isolation for thousands of years. The beaches are the best in Europe. The interior is dramatic and barely visited. The food is extraordinary and almost entirely unknown outside the island. This is not a destination that rewards short visits or half-attention.

Why Sardinia Is Different

Sardinia has been inhabited continuously for at least 12,000 years. The Nuragic civilisation — which built over 7,000 mysterious stone towers (nuraghi) between 1800 and 500 BC — left a prehistoric landscape unlike anything in Europe. The Romans, Phoenicians, Spaniards, and Genoese all left their mark without ever fully absorbing the island's distinct identity. The result is a place that feels simultaneously Italian and deeply its own thing.

The landscape ranges from the white-sand, turquoise-water coastline of the north to the granite peaks and oak forests of the Gennargentu mountains in the interior — a part of Sardinia that receives almost no tourists and where traditional pastoral life continues largely unchanged. Blue Zones researchers have identified the Nuoro province as one of the world's five regions with the highest concentration of centenarians. The Sardinian diet — pecorino, bread, beans, olive oil, and Cannonau wine — is cited as a contributing factor.

Europe's Most Beautiful Coastline

The waters off Sardinia's northeast coast contain some of the clearest and most vividly coloured sea in the Mediterranean. The Costa Smeralda — "the Emerald Coast" — earned its name honestly: the water shifts from jade to sapphire to turquoise depending on the depth, the time of day, and the angle of the light.

The Best Beaches in Sardinia

Sardinia has over 1,800km of coastline and hundreds of beaches. These are the ones that justify the journey.

Top Rated

Cala Goloritzé

Accessible only by boat or a 2-hour mountain hike — a natural arch of white limestone, perfect turquoise water, and a natural monument designation that limits visitor numbers. One of Italy's most beautiful beaches, full stop.

Most Famous

La Pelosa

The postcard image of Sardinia — shallow turquoise water, white sand, a medieval watchtower. Entry is now ticketed and limited to 1,500 visitors per day to protect the ecosystem. Book weeks ahead in summer.

Best in South

Chia Beaches

A string of sand-dune beaches in the southwest, each with a different character. Su Giudeu and Spiaggia di Chia are the finest — shallow water, flamingos in the lagoon behind, and far fewer crowds than the Costa Smeralda.

Wild East

Cala Luna

A dramatic cove in the Golfo di Orosei, backed by caves and pink oleander flowers. Reachable by boat from Cala Gonone or by a 2.5-hour coastal hike. Wild, remote, and extraordinary.

Family Favourite

Villasimius

The southeastern resort town with some of the finest beaches in southern Sardinia — Spiaggia del Riso and Campus are the standouts. A marine protected area keeps the water pristine. Good infrastructure without being overdeveloped.

Luxury Coast

Costa Smeralda

The Aga Khan's creation — Europe's most exclusive coastal resort, centred on Porto Cervo. The beaches (Liscia Ruja, Capriccioli) are genuinely spectacular. Accommodation is very expensive; visit as a day trip from Olbia or Palau.

Sardinia's Regions — Where to Go

Cagliari — The Capital

Sardinia's ancient capital deserves more time than most visitors give it. The Castello district perches on a limestone hill above the city — Pisan towers, baroque churches, and views over the Golfo degli Angeli. The botanic garden and the National Archaeological Museum are both excellent. Poetto beach runs for 8km just outside the centre.

Alghero — The Catalan Town

Alghero is linguistically and architecturally Catalan — colonised by Aragonese in the 14th century, it still has street signs in Catalan (Alguerès). The old town sits on a promontory surrounded by sea walls; the surrounding coastline (Porto Conte, Neptune's Grotto) is some of the finest on the island.

Nuoro & the Barbagia

The rugged mountain interior that most tourists fly over. The Barbagia — historically the most isolated region of Sardinia — is where ancient traditions survive: Mamuthones carnival masks, hand-woven textiles, and the cuisine that has produced the most centenarians in Europe. Not easy to reach without a car, but deeply rewarding.

Olbia & the North

The main gateway to the Costa Smeralda. Olbia itself is a useful base rather than a destination — fly in, pick up your car, and head to the coast. The Maddalena Archipelago (a national park of tiny islands with extraordinary water) is accessible by ferry from Palau, 30 minutes north.

The Nuraghi — 7,000 Prehistoric Towers

Sardinia's most extraordinary secret: more than 7,000 stone towers built between 1800 and 500 BC by a civilisation that left no written records. Su Nuraxi di Barumini (UNESCO, near Cagliari) is the finest and most accessible example. Wandering among the Bronze Age ruins on a quiet morning, with no other visitors, is a genuinely profound experience.

Beyond the Beach — What Else to Do

Hiking the Supramonte

The limestone massif of the Supramonte near Orgosolo is one of Italy's finest hiking landscapes — dramatic gorges, prehistoric rock paintings, wild mountain springs, and a silence that feels ancient. The Gola di Gorropu (the "Grand Canyon of Europe") is a 500-metre deep limestone gorge accessible by a 3-hour round-trip hike.

Hire a local guide for the more remote routes — the Supramonte is vast, trails are unmarked, and the terrain is genuinely challenging. The Cooperativa Gorropu in Urzulei organises excellent guided walks.

The Maddalena Archipelago

Take the 20-minute ferry from Palau to La Maddalena island — then hire a boat to reach the smaller islands of Budelli (home to the famous pink-sand Spiaggia Rosa, now a protected nature reserve, viewable from the water) and Spargi. The water in the archipelago rivals anything in the Caribbean for clarity and colour.

Sardinian Food & Wine

Sardinian cuisine is one of Italy's most distinct regional traditions — shaped by centuries of inland pastoral life rather than coastal fishing, despite being an island. The food is rustic, ancient, and extraordinary.

Porceddu

Roast suckling pig — the centrepiece of Sardinian feast cooking. Cooked over myrtle wood for several hours.

Bottarga

Dried and cured mullet roe — grated over pasta, eaten thin-sliced with bread. The "truffle of the sea."

Pecorino Sardo

Sardinia's sheep's cheese — aged and sharp, or young and mild. Buy from a local shop, not a supermarket.

Cannonau Wine

Sardinia's indigenous red grape — one of the world's oldest. Rich, warm, and extraordinarily food-friendly.

  • Malloreddus: Sardinia's signature pasta — small ridged gnocchi-like shapes made from semolina, served with saffron and sausage ragù (alla Campidanese). Order it everywhere.
  • Pane Carasau: The ancient Sardinian flatbread — crisp, thin, and extraordinary with olive oil and salt or used to scoop up stews. Every Sardinian meal begins with it.
  • Mirto: A liqueur made from myrtle berries — the digestivo of choice after every meal. Buy a bottle to take home.
  • Where to eat: Avoid any restaurant in a beach resort in July–August that posts prices in English on a chalkboard outside. Inland towns have better, cheaper food. Ask your accommodation for recommendations.

Getting There & Around

Three airports serve Sardinia: Cagliari (CAG) in the south, Olbia (OLB) in the northeast (closest to Costa Smeralda), and Alghero (AHO) in the northwest. Ryanair and easyJet fly direct from many European cities; Alitalia/ITA operates from Rome and Milan. The ferry from Civitavecchia (Rome) to Olbia or Cagliari takes 7–11 hours — a pleasant overnight option with a cabin.

Rent a car. This cannot be overstated. Sardinia is large (roughly the size of Wales or New Jersey), public buses between towns run infrequently, and the best beaches, nuraghi, and interior landscapes are completely inaccessible without your own transport. Book a car before you arrive — rental desks at Sardinian airports sell out in peak season.

Practical Tips

  • Avoid July and August unless you book everything months in advance. Prices double, the best beaches are overcrowded, and accommodation near the coast becomes unavailable. May, June, September and early October offer the same warm water with a fraction of the crowds.
  • La Pelosa beach requires an online ticket reservation (€3.50) from June 15 to September 15 — numbers are strictly limited to protect the fragile ecosystem. Book at lapolosastintino.it.
  • The Golfo di Orosei (east coast) beaches are accessible primarily by boat — rent one from Cala Gonone harbour or join a boat tour. This is one of Sardinia's great experiences and costs €30–50 per person for a day.
  • Sardinian drivers treat rural roads as personal property. Drive carefully on mountain roads, pull fully off the road when stopping, and don't rush.
  • Download offline maps before arriving — mobile signal is patchy in the interior and along many coastal routes.
